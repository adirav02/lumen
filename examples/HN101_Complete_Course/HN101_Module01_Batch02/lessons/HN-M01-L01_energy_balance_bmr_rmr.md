# Lesson 1.1 — Energy Balance and Metabolic Measurement: BMR versus RMR

## 1. Purpose and learning outcomes

How can someone eat more than their resting metabolic rate and still lose weight? Why does a higher reading on a metabolic test not necessarily indicate a lasting metabolic change? This lesson builds the quantitative and methodological foundations needed to answer both questions.

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Convert between kilocalories and kilojoules and distinguish energy from a rate of energy expenditure.
2. Calculate daily and cumulative energy balance and interpret its sign.
3. Explain why a change in scale weight does not directly measure a change in stored energy.
4. Distinguish dietary fat, adipose tissue, and skeletal muscle tissue.
5. Evaluate whether a described measurement protocol supports a BMR or RMR interpretation.
6. Separate measurements, predictions, assumptions, and unsupported conclusions.

Suggested study time: 25 minutes of reading, 15 minutes of worked examples, 15 minutes in the scenario lab, and 20 minutes for the assessment.

Scope: general education for healthy adults. Numerical cases are synthetic teaching examples, not personal nutrition prescriptions. The practical component is a text-based scenario lab with feedback; this document does not contain an executable interactive application.

## 2. Opening problem: a correct subtraction can answer the wrong question

A trainee says:

> “I ate 2,300 calories yesterday, and my watch reported 600 activity calories. Therefore, I had an energy surplus of 1,700 calories.”

The subtraction is arithmetically correct. The interpretation is not established. Before using the numbers, ask:

- Does “activity calories” mean total daily expenditure or only part of it?
- Do both numbers cover the same time period?
- Were they measured, predicted, or self-reported?

A useful habit throughout this course is to define a number before calculating with it.

## 3. Energy, calories, and rates

### 3.1 Units

In nutrition, “calorie” usually refers to a kilocalorie, abbreviated kcal. We will use explicit units:

\[
1\ \mathrm{kcal}=1000\ \mathrm{cal}=4.184\ \mathrm{kJ}
\]

For example:

\[
500\ \mathrm{kcal}\times4.184\ \frac{\mathrm{kJ}}{\mathrm{kcal}}=2092\ \mathrm{kJ}
\]

The units change; the amount of energy does not.

### 3.2 An amount is different from a rate

“1,600 kcal” describes an amount of energy. “1,600 kcal/day” describes a rate expressed on a daily basis.

A metabolic test can measure a resting rate over a shorter interval and express that rate in kcal/day. This does not mean that the test measured every activity performed over an entire day. Extrapolating a resting rate to daily units also does not transform it into total daily expenditure.

**Concept check HN-M01-L01-C01:** A hypothetical expenditure rate remains at 70 kcal/hour for 24 hours. Calculate total energy expenditure and state the assumption that makes the calculation possible.

## 4. The energy-balance framework

For this introductory lesson, consider an adult who is neither pregnant nor lactating:

\[
\Delta E_{stores}=E_{in}-E_{out}
\]

Here, E_in is metabolizable dietary energy available over the interval, E_out is total energy expenditure over the same interval, and ΔE_stores is the net change in stored body energy. Food labels and intake logs estimate E_in; they are not perfect direct measurements of an individual's absorbed energy. [S1, S3]

| Result | Interpretation within the model |
| --- | --- |
| ΔE is positive | Net stored body energy increases. |
| ΔE is negative | Net stored body energy decreases. |
| ΔE is zero | There is no net change in stored body energy over the interval. |

All terms must cover the same time window. Subtracting the expenditure of one workout from a week of food intake does not produce a weekly energy balance.

### Worked example HN-M01-L01-E01

Suppose daily intake is 2,400 kcal and total daily expenditure is 2,700 kcal:

\[
\Delta E=2400-2700=-300\ \mathrm{kcal}
\]

If these exact values persist for seven days:

\[
\Delta E_{week}=7\times(-300)=-2100\ \mathrm{kcal}
\]

This is a conditional calculation of energy change. It is not an exact prediction of a person's change in kilograms.

### 4.1 What CICO explains

“Calories In, Calories Out” describes energy accounting. By itself, it does not explain appetite, adherence, changes in movement, or the composition of gained or lost tissue. Intake and expenditure may change during an intervention; dynamic body-weight models account for changing expenditure and body composition. [S3]

The equation tells us how energy quantities relate. Predicting an individual's trajectory additionally requires estimates, assumptions, and observations.

## 5. Why scale weight is not a direct energy measurement

Body mass includes tissues, water, glycogen, and gastrointestinal contents. Changes in these components need not occur at the same rate. Consequently, one short-term weight change does not identify the composition or energy content of the change. [S4]

### 5.1 Dietary fat is not identical to adipose tissue

| Term | Meaning | Invalid inference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dietary fat | A macronutrient with a general food-energy factor of approximately 9 kcal/g. | Every kilogram of adipose tissue contains exactly 9,000 kcal. |
| Adipose tissue | Tissue containing stored lipids and other constituents. | Every kilogram lost from the body has its energy density. |
| Skeletal muscle tissue | Tissue containing substantial water, proteins, and other components. | The food-energy factor for protein gives the energy cost of building a kilogram of muscle. |

General food-energy factors are approximately 4 kcal/g for protein and carbohydrate and 9 kcal/g for fat. These are average dietary conversion factors, not body-composition equations. [S1]

The energy density of tissue change depends on what changes. Energy retained in new tissue and the expenditure involved in producing that tissue are also different quantities. [S3, S4]

### 5.2 The limitation of the “7,700 kcal per kilogram” shortcut

A fixed energy-per-kilogram rule cannot provide a universally accurate, linear prediction of body-weight change. Tissue composition and expenditure can change over time. [S3]

**Worked interpretation HN-M01-L01-E02:** A person's scale weight rises from 80.0 to 80.8 kg in one day. This observation alone does not establish a gain of 0.8 kg of fat or permit an exact calculation of an energy surplus.

When a question provides insufficient information, identifying that limitation is a valid scientific answer.

## 6. BMR, RMR, and TDEE

### 6.1 Basal metabolic rate: BMR

BMR describes energy expenditure under standardized basal conditions. FAO/WHO/UNU describes measurement conditions including being awake, lying supine, fasting for 10–12 hours, having prior physical rest, being mentally relaxed, and remaining in a thermoneutral environment. Thermoneutral conditions avoid a substantial requirement for heat production or dissipation. Essential physiological processes continue during rest. [S1]

The key point is methodological: “basal” refers to specified conditions, not merely to someone being inactive.

### 6.2 Resting metabolic rate: RMR

RMR describes expenditure at rest. The term is often applied to measurements obtained under conditions less restrictive than strict basal conditions. The actual protocol matters. Recent food intake, prior exercise, and inadequate rest can compromise interpretation as resting expenditure. Quietly sitting down does not automatically establish a valid RMR measurement. [S2]

This lesson does not use a universal conversion factor between BMR and RMR. A standard deviation for their difference would require an appropriate dataset, not merely a checklist of measurement conditions.

### 6.3 Total daily energy expenditure: TDEE

TDEE covers total expenditure over a day, including resting expenditure, food-related expenditure, and physical activity. A resting estimate is therefore different from total daily expenditure. A device's “activity calories” may represent only one component; its definition must be checked. [S1, S2]

| Feature | BMR | RMR | TDEE |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Question | What is expenditure under basal conditions? | What is expenditure under the stated resting protocol? | What is total expenditure over a day? |
| Context | Strict standardized conditions. | Resting conditions defined by the protocol. | All daily components. |
| Common reporting unit | kcal/day | kcal/day | kcal/day |
| Sufficient by itself for a personal intake prescription? | No. | No. | Individual goals and context are still needed. |

### 6.4 Measurement versus prediction

Indirect calorimetry estimates expenditure from respiratory gas exchange. A prediction equation uses characteristics such as body mass and age within a model derived from observed data. These produce different kinds of estimates, each with limitations. [S2, S5]

Before interpreting a value, ask: **What quantity does it represent, how was it obtained, and under what conditions?**

Lesson 1.2 will introduce prediction equations. Here, the priority is interpreting their outputs correctly.

## 7. Scenario lab HN-M01-L01-LAB01

### Instructions

Classify each scenario using the best-supported option:

- **A:** Conditions are consistent with a basal measurement, subject to valid measurement procedures.
- **B:** Conditions support a resting measurement; strict basal conditions have not been established.
- **C:** An explicit protocol problem prevents an uncomplicated basal/resting interpretation.
- **D:** Information is insufficient to classify the result.

This is an educational classification exercise, not a clinical testing protocol. A checklist cannot numerically correct a post-meal measurement or automatically convert it into BMR.

| Scenario ID | Description |
| --- | --- |
| LAB01-01 | A participant stays overnight in the laboratory, fasts for 12 hours, rests overnight, and is measured awake, supine, relaxed, and thermoneutral. Equipment is calibrated and a stable measurement segment is obtained. |
| LAB01-02 | An outpatient test follows the laboratory's documented RMR protocol, with no recent meal or exercise, adequate documented rest, and thermoneutral conditions. Strict basal conditions were not established. |
| LAB01-03 | A test labeled “RMR” starts immediately after breakfast and a cycling workout. |
| LAB01-04 | A report says only “BMR: 1,680,” without units, measurement method, or test conditions. |

### Change one variable

Starting from LAB01-01, make each change separately:

1. Add a meal one hour before measurement.
2. Replace the thermoneutral environment with a room cold enough to induce shivering.
3. Replace the instrument output with a prediction-equation result.

For each change, explain what becomes problematic: basal conditions, comparability, or the claim that this is an individual measurement. Do not invent a calorie adjustment. Identifying an influence does not quantify its magnitude.

### Paired-data calculation

For arithmetic practice only, suppose valid tests on the same person report BMR of 1,600 and RMR of 1,680 kcal/day:

\[
\text{Relative difference from BMR}=\frac{1680-1600}{1600}\times100=5\%
\]

The result describes this pair relative to its BMR value. It is not a conversion rule for other people. Dividing by RMR instead would answer a different percentage question, so always identify the reference value.

## 8. Independent practice

**HN-M01-L01-P01 — Accumulation:** For five days, intake is 2,500 kcal/day and expenditure is 2,800. On the other two days, intake is 3,100 and expenditure is 2,700. Calculate weekly balance and average daily balance.

**HN-M01-L01-P02 — Interpretation:** Return to the opening problem. Explain why the stated 1,700 kcal surplus is unsupported. Identify the missing quantity.

**HN-M01-L01-P03 — Data criticism:** A calculator predicts RMR of 1,750 kcal/day; a laboratory test reports 1,900. Identify two things to investigate before concluding that one result is wrong.

## 9. Unit assessment HN-M01-L01-EX01

Time: 20 minutes. Total: 100 points. A calculator is permitted. Numerical inputs are exact teaching values unless stated otherwise. Each multiple-choice question has one correct answer.

### Q01 — Unit conversion — 10 points

250 kcal is equivalent to:

A. 250 kJ.
B. 1,046 kJ.
C. 59.8 kJ.
D. 250,000 kJ.

### Q02 — Definition — 10 points

TDEE means:

A. Workout expenditure only.
B. Expenditure measured under fasting conditions only.
C. Total energy expenditure over a day.
D. Energy consumed in the most recent meal.

### Q03 — Measurement conditions — 10 points

Which statement about BMR is correct?

A. The participant must be asleep during measurement.
B. Measurement requires standardized basal conditions, including wakefulness and rest.
C. Fasting is the only relevant condition.
D. Any online calculator directly measures BMR.

### Q04 — Inference — 10 points

Scale weight increases by 0.7 kg in one day. What follows from that observation alone?

A. Exactly 0.7 kg of fat was gained.
B. A surplus of exactly 5,390 kcal occurred.
C. Exactly 0.7 kg of muscle was gained.
D. Recorded body mass increased; the composition of the change is unknown.

### Q05 — Cumulative balance — 20 points

For three days, E_in is 2,200 kcal/day and E_out is 2,600. For four days, E_in is 2,700 and E_out is 2,500. Calculate cumulative balance and average daily balance. Interpret the sign. Can you determine the exact body-weight change?

### Q06 — Hidden assumptions — 20 points

Given only a measured BMR of 1,600 kcal/day, a student states that RMR is 1,760 and TDEE is 2,400. Identify two assumptions not supplied by the data. Explain why this is not a set of three measurements.

### Q07 — Methodological critique — 20 points

LAB01-03 produces 1,950 and LAB01-01 produces 1,700 kcal/day. A researcher claims: “The difference proves that basal metabolism improved by 250 calories per day.” Identify the comparison problem, explain why the difference does not establish a basal change, and propose a better comparison.

## 10. Practical assignment

Choose one value from a calculator, a report, or a wearable. Record what it claims to represent, its units, its time window, how it was obtained, and what information is missing. A fictional value is acceptable. No diet change or laboratory test is required.

Submit a paragraph of up to 150 words titled **“What does this number allow me to conclude?”**

## 11. References and evidence map

Explanations, questions, and numerical scenarios were written for this course. Scenario values are not research findings. Source type matters: measurement standards define procedures; prediction studies do not guarantee individual accuracy; dynamic models do not directly measure a particular person.

- **S1 — FAO/WHO/UNU. Human Energy Requirements (2004), Chapter 2.** Official expert report supporting units, general dietary energy factors, expenditure components, and basal conditions. [Source](https://www.fao.org/4/y5686e/y5686e04.htm).
- **S2 — Chen et al. (2020). Room Indirect Calorimetry Operating and Reporting Standards (RICORS 1.0).** Methods and standards supporting measurement definitions and conditions. [Source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7526647/).
- **S3 — Hall et al. (2011). Quantification of the effect of energy imbalance on bodyweight.** Dynamic modeling study supporting limitations of static weight-change predictions. [Source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3880593/).
- **S4 — Hall et al. (2012). Energy balance and its components: implications for body weight regulation.** Scientific consensus report supporting distinctions among energy, mass, and body composition. [Source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3302369/).
- **S5 — Mifflin et al. (1990). A new predictive equation for resting energy expenditure in healthy individuals.** Prediction-equation development study; detailed coverage follows in Lesson 1.2. [Source](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2305711/).
